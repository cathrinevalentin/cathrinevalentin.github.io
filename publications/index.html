  • Kjær, C. V. & Pozsar, H. Online Political Influence: The Role of Groups.

    Abstract. When large-scale social media accounts engage in political discussion, do they influence their audiences, and if so, through what mechanisms? Existing literature attributes the influence of these accounts primarily to parasocial relationships between actors and their audiences. This explanation overlooks a key feature of digital spaces: the reactions of others, instantly visible through likes, comments, and reshares. We therefore test two mechanisms experimentally: whether parasocial relationships drive political influence, and whether the visible reactions of fellow followers condition such effects. Our central contribution is a novel experimental design that uses targeted social media advertising to recruit the existing followers of a real, prominent social media actor: members of the online in-group around Taylor Swift. The design activates real, pre-existing parasocial relationships rather than constructing artificial ones, and reaches participants in the setting where online influence would ordinarily occur. Holding pre-existing parasocial attachment constant, we experimentally vary exposure to political content from Taylor Swift and to the visible reactions of online in-group members, addressing the challenge of self-selection. We find no support for either a parasocial effect or a conditional effect of in-group signals. Although the study is not powered to detect very small effects, the results suggest that parasocial and group-based persuasion online may be weaker than commonly assumed, directing the search for political influence toward other mechanisms and other levels of analysis.

    Presented at the APSA Annual Meeting & Exhibition, 2025. Panel: “Social Media Engagement and Political Expression,” the Political Behavior Colloquium, European University Institute, 2026, and the Political Behavior Workshop, Aarhus University, 2026.

  • Kjær, C. V. When Self-Correction Fails: Selective Expression and Extreme Comment Climates on Social Media

    Abstract. Under what conditions can self-reinforcing dynamics persist in interactive social media environments? This paper resolves a tension in the existing literature. Self-correction arguments predict that early extreme signals should dilute over time because subsequent contributions reflect the broader distribution of attitudes in the population. Only sufficiently strong influence on observers' attitudes can prevent this dilution. Yet experimental studies consistently find that influence effects are small, while digital environments are frequently associated with collective outcomes that amplify rather than dilute extreme signals, including misinformation cascades and hate speech. I argue that this puzzle arises because existing models implicitly assume universal participation. On social media, most users remain silent, and the visible comment climate is instead produced by an unrepresentative and typically more extreme minority of users. I term this pattern \textit{selective expression} and show, using an agent-based model, that selective expression alone is sufficient to break self-correction. Whereas the canonical model converges toward the population mean, selective expression causes the visible comment climate to converge toward one of two extreme equilibria. This result holds even when attitudes remain unchanged by the visible climate; allowing modest influence only strengthens the pattern. The paper therefore identifies selective expression as a scope condition for self-correcting influence processes: self-correction depends not only on interactivity, but also on who participates. It demonstrates how the behaviour of an otherwise invisible silent majority can shape collective outcomes in digital environments.

    This project traces the educational and professional pathways of mid-level actors in AI and quantum governance to map the imaginaries, shared ideas about technology's transformative potential, that shape how these fields are governed.

  • Kjær, C. V. & Pozsar, H. Banning Mobile Phones in Schools and Student Wellbeing

    This paper exploits a staggered parent-led rollout of mobile phone bans during the school day in Danish schools, using a difference-in-differences design to estimate their causal effect on student wellbeing.
